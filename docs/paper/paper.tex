\begin{abstract}
Industrial automated guided vehicles (AGVs) require reliable Wi\nobreakdash-Fi connectivity for safety telemetry, traffic management, mission updates, and remote intervention. However, signal strength in industrial workspaces is hard to predict from the access point (AP) distance alone due to waveguiding, blockage, and multipath. Since industrial AGVs are equipped with safety LiDARs that capture environmental geometry, the natural hypothesis is that LiDAR-derived features can improve Wi\nobreakdash-Fi signal strength prediction over what AP-relative geometry alone provides. This paper presents a use-case study of LiDAR-augmented signal strength prediction on data gathered from a deployed industrial AGV (three routes, 681,593 matched samples). Two evaluation protocols are used: cross-session and within-session generalization. Five robustness controls are applied: an XGBoost hyperparameter sweep, a LightGBM framework cross-check, a 1-meter spatial buffer-zone exclusion, a depth-constrained overfitting check, and LiDAR-feature permutation tests. Across the configurations tested, ego-frame LiDAR features did not contribute robust value over a 10-feature baseline of position, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry; the only nominal positive (the F3 cross-session fold, trained and tested across independently-aligned reference frames) was diagnosed by the permutation control as marginal-distribution-driven rather than row-aligned LiDAR signal. The lowest-RMSE LiDAR-free model achieves an RMSE of 3.96 dB on \texttt{signal\_power}, approximately 1 dB below the dataset's noise floor. The paper presents the first empirical study of LiDAR-augmented Wi\nobreakdash-Fi signal strength prediction for industrial AGVs and identifies a minimal feature set sufficient for sub-noise-floor within-session prediction in the studied scenario.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

With the ongoing adoption of Industry 4.0 and 5.0 paradigms, automated guided vehicles (AGVs) are increasingly deployed in warehouse, manufacturing, and distribution environments~\cite{fragapane2021planning}. Reliable wireless communication supports the coordination functions of AGV fleets, including safety telemetry, traffic management, mission updates, and remote intervention. Thus, unpredictable signal quality can result in idle time, near-collisions, or unplanned safety stops. Wi\nobreakdash-Fi remains the dominant choice for these links due to its cost, infrastructure availability, and coverage characteristics, but the conditions under which industrial Wi\nobreakdash-Fi performs reliably differ from those of office or residential installations~\cite{cena2025wifi,schmieder2023agvdatasets}. Metallic shelving, structural columns, loading equipment, and moving forklifts produce a propagation environment dominated by waveguiding, blockage, and multipath. Empirical log-distance models, even with precisely measured access point (AP) coordinates, fit indoor industrial measurements with considerably more residual variance than outdoor or campus deployments~\cite{cena2025wifi,formis2025wifi}. Field-deployable predictors of received signal strength along a planned AGV trajectory are therefore operationally valuable: they let the fleet coordination software pre-compute coverage, flag risk zones, and schedule jobs around predicted dropout regions~\cite{gulia2025evowisva}.

Industrial AGVs compliant with ISO 3691-4~\cite{iso3691-4} are typically equipped with 2D LiDARs for personnel detection, protective field monitoring, and on-board localization. These scanners continuously produce a wide-angle range scan of the AGV's local environment, capturing corridor geometry, blockage along arbitrary directions, and clutter density. Indoor Wi\nobreakdash-Fi propagation is primarily shaped by these geometric properties, which motivates the question of whether LiDAR-derived environmental features can improve signal strength prediction beyond what AP-relative geometry alone provides, and whether the added information justifies the integration effort. Recent work has shown that point-cloud-derived environmental features can improve mmWave-band channel prediction~\cite{ohta2023pointcloud,nist2024mmwave,zhang2025mmlscm,clark2022propeml}. Existing AGV Wi\nobreakdash-Fi link quality predictors~\cite{formis2025wifi,formis2024deep,colletto2023ann} use telemetry alone and do not incorporate environmental geometry. To our knowledge, the intersection of LiDAR-derived environmental features, Wi\nobreakdash-Fi link quality prediction, and industrial AGV deployment has not been explored.

This paper presents a use-case study, conducted on data gathered from a deployed industrial AGV across three routes, that examines whether ego-frame LiDAR features improve over a baseline that uses only AGV position, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry. 
The paper addresses the following research questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1.} Do LiDAR-derived environment features improve received signal strength prediction over a baseline of AGV position, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry, in cross-session generalization?
    \item \textbf{RQ2.} Do LiDAR-derived environment features improve prediction in within-session leave-region-out (LRO) generalization, after controls for spatial autocorrelation and tree-depth overfitting?
    \item \textbf{RQ3.} Are the observed effects robust to feature group substitution, hyperparameter and framework choice, and to a LiDAR-feature permutation control?
\end{itemize}

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The first controlled empirical evaluation of LiDAR-augmented Wi\nobreakdash-Fi signal strength prediction on data gathered from a deployed industrial AGV is performed.
    \item A feature group ablation procedure is performed that separates the case where a feature group provides no useful information from the case where it is substitutable for another retained group.
    \item A minimal feature set is identified that is sufficient for accurate within-session signal strength prediction in the studied scenario.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:rw} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset, its preparation, and the feature stack. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the experimental methodology. Sections~\ref{sec:cross-session} and~\ref{sec:within-session} report the cross-session and within-session experiments, respectively. Section~\ref{sec:shap} presents the feature attribution analysis. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:rw}

A coherent body of work on industrial Wi\nobreakdash-Fi link quality prediction has used telemetry-only features without explicitly modeling the surrounding workspace. Linear combinations of exponential moving averages~\cite{formis2023moving} and shallow neural networks~\cite{colletto2023ann,scanzio2022ann} reach competitive accuracy on warehouse-deployed equipment, and recent extensions replace shallow models with deep architectures~\cite{formis2024deep,formis2025wifi}; the feature inventory across these works remains essentially telemetric. Broader surveys on machine learning for IEEE 802.11~\cite{wilhelmi2024aiml,szott2022wifimeetsml} catalog similar feature sets and identify environmental modeling as an under-investigated direction, particularly in industrial deployments where waveguiding and blockage dominate. The fundamental challenges of Wi\nobreakdash-Fi reliability for moving equipment in such environments are also documented in~\cite{cena2025wifi}. The cumulative picture is that telemetry features alone leave residual variance that prior state-of-the-art does not account for, motivating the search for additional features that capture environmental structure.

The use of geometric environmental features for radio-channel prediction has been explored most extensively in the millimeter-wave (mmWave) band. The work~\cite{ohta2023pointcloud} uses raw LiDAR point clouds to anticipate line-of-sight blockage by pedestrians in 60\,GHz wireless LAN, demonstrating that point cloud-based prediction is feasible at indoor scales without the privacy concerns of camera-based methods. The authors of~\cite{nist2024mmwave} fuse LiDAR scans with panoramic imagery in a PointNet-based regressor for prediction of mmWave channel parameters, outperforming uncalibrated 3GPP indoor-hotspot baselines.
A complementary approach in~\cite{zhang2025mmlscm} introduces a multi-modal neural radio radiance field that combines reference signal received power (RSRP) measurements with LiDAR point clouds for localized statistical channel modeling, reporting improved reconstruction accuracy and noise robustness over RSRP-only baselines. Outside the mmWave band, the PropEM-L framework~\cite{clark2022propeml} encodes raw point clouds for communication-aware multi-robot exploration and reports up to 44\% improvement in received signal strength prediction over a log-distance baseline in subterranean environments. GPU-accelerated point-cloud ray tracing has also been demonstrated as a complementary technique for outdoor channel prediction~\cite{pang2021gpuraytrace}. The reported gains across these works are substantial, but the propagation regimes differ from the 2.4/5\,GHz indoor industrial case: mmWave is dominated by direct-path attenuation and discrete blockage events, whereas industrial Wi\nobreakdash-Fi is shaped by waveguiding through corridors and constructive/destructive multipath at length scales much larger than typical LiDAR feature scales. Whether geometric features that succeed in mmWave or in subterranean settings transfer to industrial Wi\nobreakdash-Fi is therefore an open empirical question.

Dedicated LiDAR/Wi\nobreakdash-Fi sensor-fusion work in the AGV domain has so far targeted localization, SLAM, mapping, and navigation drift compensation~\cite{li2025lidaragv,nguyen2022wifilidar}, not link quality prediction. The above literature review reveals a gap at the intersection of LiDAR-derived environmental features, Wi\nobreakdash-Fi link quality prediction, and industrial AGV deployment. The use-case study presented in this paper aims to address that gap by providing the experimental controls necessary to support a credible deployment-relevant conclusion.

\subsection{Time synchronization}
LiDAR scans and telemetry samples originate from independent hardware and software stacks with their own clocks. Even moderate temporal drift between the two streams biases any subsequent learning, because LiDAR-derived features would describe a slightly different position from the one the telemetry suggests. Time alignment is, therefore, a prerequisite for the use-case study and is performed before any feature engineering. 
A per-session offset $\hat{\tau}$ is estimated as the value that maximizes the cross-correlation between a telemetry signature and a LiDAR-side scan-to-scan dissimilarity signal, computed on a common 50\,Hz grid with parabolic peak refinement. The estimation is performed once per session, and the resulting confidence interval is quantified via the moving-block bootstrap. Fig.~\ref{fig:timesync} shows the cross-correlation curve for one session and the estimated peak.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{docs/paper/img/timesync_xcorr.png}
    \caption{Time synchronization between LiDAR and telemetry streams: (a) normalized cross-correlation between the telemetry signature and the LiDAR scan-to-scan dissimilarity signal; (b) local
    shape of each peak, centered at the corresponding $\hat{\tau}$. Positive $\tau$ corresponds to LiDAR lagging telemetry.}
    \label{fig:timesync}
\end{figure}

The hypothesis that a single offset applies to all three sessions is tested against the per-session estimates with Cochran's $Q$ test ($Q = 8.06$, $df = 2$, $p = 0.018$); the test rejects the equal-offsets hypothesis, so per-session offsets are used.  Within each session, two complementary stability tests support the use of a single constant offset. A half-split re-estimation, in which $\hat{\tau}$ is recomputed independently on the first and second half of each session, agrees within 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) on all three sessions, with a maximum half-to-half $|\Delta\hat{\tau}| = 0.178$\,s on S1. A sliding-window drift fit, in which $\hat{\tau}$ is recomputed on overlapping 5--10\,min windows and regressed against window-center wall-clock time, yields per-session slopes between $-1.4$ and $-0.6$\,ms/min ($p \ge 0.075$ on every session), with the accumulated within-session drift bounded by each session's bootstrap $\sigma_{\text{RMSE}}$ on $\hat{\tau}$.



The estimated offsets, applied as constant per-session shifts to align the two streams, are $\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{S}1} = +0.250$\,s, $\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{S}2} = +0.432$\,s, and $\hat{\tau}_{\mathrm{S}3} = +0.879$\,s. After alignment and joining, 97.0\% of LiDAR scans (681{,}593 of 702{,}624) have a successfully matched telemetry sample and are retained in the training-ready dataset. 



\subsection{Anomaly handling}
\label{sec:anomaly}
The downstream models use the AGV's reported position $(x_m, y_m)$ to compute every AP-relative feature. When on-board localization struggles—typically during initialization, after a manual repositioning of the AGV, in feature-poor environments, or under sensor degradation—the reported position becomes unreliable, and so do the features derived from it. Such rows must be excluded so that the feature derivation remains physically meaningful.

The NNS exposes a position-confidence score \texttt{nns\_position\_confidence} on a 0--100 scale, with a high value indicating that NNS estimates its own pose to be reliable. The distribution of this score across the entire dataset is provided in Fig.~\ref{fig:confidence-hist}.

\subsection{Dataset noise floor}
\label{sec:noisefloor}
Reported model accuracy values are interpretable only against a reference for the irreducible variability of the data. We therefore characterize the dataset noise floor at the spatial resolution at which features are realistically deployable, namely 0.5\,m position binning: if two visits to the same 0.5-m cell at different times yield substantially different \texttt{signal\_power} readings, no model that uses 0.5-m position binning alone can resolve below that variability.

The noise-floor estimate uses the 85 cells of size 0.5\,m that contain at least 30 rows in both S1 and S2 (the two sessions sharing the same coordinate reference frame, allowing same-cell repeat measurements). The mean per-cell standard deviation of \texttt{signal\_power} within a session is 4.90\,dB on S1, 5.01\,dB on S2, and 4.95\,dB pooled. The median absolute difference between the two sessions' per-cell mean values is 4.24\,dB. We therefore take 4.95\,dB as the noise floor at 0.5-m position binning. Any predictor that resolves below this floor must be exploiting information at finer spatial granularity than 0.5\,m --- for instance, position at sensor resolution, telemetry, or AP-relative geometry.

\subsection{Feature stack}
\label{sec:features}
The training-ready dataset comprises 29 numeric features, organized into five feature groups, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:features}.
The empirical valid LiDAR field of view (FOV) is narrower than the nominal 270$^{\circ}$. Of the 1{,}350 active beams, 241 hit the AGV body itself at sub-200\,mm range and are permanently masked. The remaining 1{,}109 beams cover a contiguous 222$^{\circ}$ sector that hosts the seven 30$^{\circ}$ sectoral aggregates. 

The experimental AGV is equipped with a single front-facing LiDAR, whereas production industrial AGVs typically use two for full 360° coverage. To account for this reduced angular coverage, evaluation metrics are stratified by \texttt{is\_AP\_in\_FOV}. The in-FOV stratum is where directional LiDAR features are most informative: the AP falls within the sensor's valid sector, and obstruction measurements toward the AP are geometrically well-defined. This stratum also serves as the dual-LiDAR-equivalent lower bound — if LiDAR-derived features contribute predictive value, it should be observable here.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Two evaluation protocols are executed. The cross-session leave-one-route-out (LORO) protocol uses three folds, each holding out one session. The held-out session contains trajectory patterns the model has not been trained on. Because the coordinate reference frames do not align across all routes, cross-session models do not use the raw position $(x_m, y_m)$; features are restricted to ego-frame LiDAR, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry.

The within-session LRO protocol uses five folds on session S1 only --- the largest session by both spatial coverage and sample count. Regions are obtained by $K$-means clustering on $(x_m, y_m)$ with $k = 5$. Within-session models do use the raw position. The two protocols together test the model's ability to transfer learned propagation structure across operating conditions (LORO) and to extrapolate to unseen physical regions of the same workspace (LRO).

The predictive model on both protocols is a gradient-boosted regression tree, trained with XGBoost (six hyperparameter configurations in total, defined below) and cross-checked with LightGBM. The target variable is the Wi\nobreakdash-Fi received signal strength, in dBm, reported for the matched LiDAR--telemetry sample.

\subsection{Feature group ablation}
\label{sec:method-disamb}
A single ablation between a full and an ablated model is ambiguous: no degradation can mean either (i) the removed feature group did not help, or (ii) the group is substitutable for another retained group. We therefore run two complementary ablations against the full model: $M^\prime$ removes feature group $A$ (here, the LiDAR features) and $M^{\prime\prime}$ removes feature group $B$ (here, the AP-relative features). The pair of resulting RMSE differences is then classified, with an absolute threshold of 0.5\,dB, into one of four verdicts: \textit{$A$ removable}, \textit{$B$ removable}, \textit{both removable}, or \textit{mixed/unclear}. The procedure is feature group-agnostic: it reports which group can be removed without loss, independently of which group the analyst expected to matter. Cross-session variants are denoted B5, B5$^\prime$, and B5$^{\prime\prime}$; within-session variants are denoted W4, W4$^\prime$, and W4$^{\prime\prime}$.

\subsection{Robustness controls}
\label{sec:method-controls}
The validity of the ablation-based comparison depends on several conditions that are tested explicitly. In what follows, the primary configuration of the XGBoost model is denoted H0 and is defined by tree depth $6$, learning rate $\eta = 0.05$, $L_2$ regularization $\lambda = 1.0$, $n_\text{est} = 2000$ boosting rounds with early-stopping after $100$ non-improving rounds, and random seed $20260427$. Validation is performed chronologically per session (last $10\,\%$ of each training session). The robustness controls, applied to the relevant fold for each diagnostic, are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Hyperparameter sensitivity.} A six-configuration sweep of the XGBoost model: H0 (the primary configuration above); H1 (depth $4$, otherwise H0); H2 (slow learning rate $\eta = 0.02$, higher regularization $\lambda = 5$); H3 (depth $8$, otherwise H0); plus H1\textsubscript{rv} and H2\textsubscript{rv} with random rather than chronological validation splits.
    \item \textit{Framework sensitivity.} A LightGBM cross-check with default and depth-equivalent hyperparameters, addressing whether the comparison is specific to XGBoost.
    \item \textit{Feature-informativeness check.} A LiDAR-feature permutation control: a per-session block-shuffle of the 19   ego-frame LiDAR columns that preserves their marginal and joint distributions but destroys row-level alignment with the target, evaluated both cross-session and within-session. If the shuffled-LiDAR model achieves the same ablation effect as the real-LiDAR model, the model was not extracting row-aligned information from the LiDAR features.
    \item \textit{Single-fold robustness.} For any within-session fold that nominally clears the threshold: a 1-m spatial buffer-zone exclusion (training rows within 1\,m of any test row are dropped) controlling spatial autocorrelation, and a depth-4 hyperparameter swap controlling tree-depth overfitting; plus their joint application.
    \item \textit{Noise-floor reference.} The noise-floor characterization of Section~\ref{sec:noisefloor} is used to contextualize the absolute RMSE values reported in   Sections~\ref{sec:cross-session} and~\ref{sec:within-session}.
\end{enumerate}

All training-set sizes after anomaly filtering are reported in Sections~\ref{sec:cross-session} and~\ref{sec:within-session}. Bootstrap 95\,\% CIs on RMSE are computed with $B = 1000$ resamples.


\section{Cross-Session Experiment}
\label{sec:cross-session}

Per-fold post-anomaly training set sizes are 337{,}390 rows for F1, 403{,}876 for F2, and 50{,}232 for F3. Six ablation variants B0--B5 progress from a distance-only baseline up to the full ego-frame LiDAR + AP-relative model (Table~\ref{tab:b-variants}).

\subsection{Spatial autocorrelation in absolute RMSE}
Within-session RMSE values cannot be read as straightforward generalization estimates: \texttt{signal\_power} is spatially autocorrelated, and the held-out region rows are typically only a few meters away from the nearest training rows. The S1 semivariogram has empirical range $\approx 20$\,m, while the $K$-means regions span 5--13\,m on a side, so training rows up to $\sim$10\,m from any held-out region row are spatially correlated with the test set. 

To bound the magnitude of this contamination, we apply a \textbf{buffer-zone test} on R-1: dropping all training rows within 1\,m of any held-out R-1 row inflates the in-FOV RMSE by $+10.21\dB$ on W0, $+10.29\dB$ on W1, $+5.63\dB$ on W2, and $+13.86\dB$ on W4. The contamination affects the LiDAR-free W2 baseline and the LiDAR-augmented W4 model differently within a fold, so the absolute RMSEs are fold-conditional rather than deployment-grade generalization estimates; the $\dlidarw$ contrast is, however, taken between models trained and tested on the same row partition, so it is not inflated by spatial autocorrelation alone. The buffer-zone correction on R-4 (Table~\ref{tab:r4-diag}) shifts $\dlidarw$ from $-1.63\dB$ (no buffer) to $-0.01\dB$ (1-m buffer): the contrast moves toward zero, never above it, under the deployment-grade buffer correction.

\subsection{R-4 robustness diagnostic}
R-4 is the smallest fold in row count, which means that it is the most exposed to spatial autocorrelation and is the fold where any latent LiDAR contribution would be most likely to surface under a relaxed-overfitting hyperparameter swap. The shuffled-feature $\dlidarw = +0.24\dB$, while the matched real-LiDAR $\dlidarw = -0.01\dB$ on the same training/validation partition: the gap is $0.25\dB$, well within the 0.5\,dB tolerance, and the shuffled fit achieves a marginally larger nominal contribution than the real fit. R-4's H0 fit therefore does not contain row-aligned LiDAR information beyond the marginal distributions of the LiDAR features. Combined with the four-correction diagnostic, R-4 supports the within-session negative result: no configuration produces a $\dlidarw$ that is both above the $+1\,$dB threshold and survives a deployment-relevant correction.

\subsection{Best-case within-session prediction accuracy}
The combination of corrections that produces the most defensible deployment-relevant estimate --- depth-4 hyperparameters with R-4 as the held-out region, in the in-FOV stratum --- gives the LiDAR-free W2 model an RMSE of $3.96\dB$ on \texttt{signal\_power}. The 10 features of W2 are AGV position $(x_m, y_m)$, three telemetry features, and five AP-relative features. The full 29-feature W4 model on the same fold and stratum achieves an RMSE of $3.36\dB$, nominally $0.60\dB$ lower (consistent with the H1-no-buffer row of Table~\ref{tab:r4-diag}); the gap does not survive the 1-m buffer correction (Section~V-C). Both numbers sit approximately 1\,dB below the 0.5-m position-binning noise floor of 4.95\,dB (Section~\ref{sec:noisefloor}), so the model's predictive power is exclusively explained by sub-cell information --- position at sensor resolution, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry --- rather than by coarse spatial binning. LiDAR features cannot contribute robust deployment-relevant information beyond what the 10-feature stack already encodes.


\section{Feature Attribution Analysis}
\label{sec:shap}

We complement the ablation-based analysis with a model-internal view using TreeSHAP. The cross-session B5 fits, and the within-session R-3 W4 fit are analyzed; R-3 is chosen for the within-session view because its overall RMSE on the leakage-fixed stack is mid-range across the five within-session folds, so its attribution patterns are representative rather than dominated by an outlier.

The cumulative $|\mathrm{SHAP}|$ over the features in each group is dominated by the AP-relative group on every fit (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap-groups}). On the cross-session folds, AP-relative contributes $6.7$--$7.7$ in cumulative $|\mathrm{SHAP}|$ across 5 features (per-feature $1.3$--$1.5$), whereas the LiDAR sectoral group contributes $1.4$--$2.0$ across 14 features (per-feature $0.10$--$0.14$). On the within-session R-3 W4 fit the AP-relative group cumulative is $6.3$ over 5 features (per-feature $1.26$) against $2.8$ over 14 for LiDAR sectoral (per-feature $0.20$). The ordering is consistent with the ablation evidence in Sections~\ref{sec:cross-session} and~\ref{sec:within-session}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{docs/paper/img/shap_group_importance.png}
    \caption{SHAP feature group importance per cross-session fold (cumulative $|\mathrm{SHAP}|$ in dB).}

We compute the Spearman correlation between each feature's value and its SHAP value, per cross-session fold, and flag a feature as sign-consistent if $|\rho| > 0.05$ with the same sign on all three cross-session folds. Of the four continuous AP-relative features tested, three pass (\texttt{dist\_to\_AP}, \texttt{sin\_AP}, \texttt{cos\_AP}); \texttt{clutter\_frac\_toward\_AP} fails (the F-B fold gives an opposite sign to F-A and F-C). The binary \texttt{is\_AP\_in\_FOV} is excluded from the rank test. Among the 19 LiDAR features (5 scalar + 14 sectoral), 5 pass (\texttt{mean\_dist\_mm}, \texttt{clutter\_frac}, \texttt{mean\_dist\_sector\_3\_mm}, \texttt{mean\_dist\_sector\_6\_mm}, \texttt{clutter\_frac\_sector\_4}). Among the 3 telemetry features, 1 passes (\texttt{speed\_mps}). The asymmetry --- $3/4$ AP-relative against $5/19$ LiDAR --- is informative: AP-relative features encode a transferable physical relationship that the gradient-boosted model picks up consistently across folds, whereas the LiDAR-derived environmental geometry does not, on this dataset.

Plotting the per-cell dominant feature group in $(x_m, y_m)$ shows that AP-relative and position together cover most of the AGV trajectory on every fold; LiDAR sectoral never dominates a contiguous spatial region. This is the per-cell counterpart of the group-importance result above and provides a direct visual
confirmation.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Industrial Wi\nobreakdash-Fi propagation is governed by waveguiding through corridors and constructive/destructive multipath at scales much larger than typical LiDAR feature scales~\cite{cena2025wifi}. The negative result is consistent with this picture: ego-frame LiDAR features over the empirical 222$^{\circ}$ valid sector capture local geometry on the meter scale. Still, the dominant propagation effects in the workspace operate over distances substantially larger than the LiDAR's sensitivity range and are not localized to the AGV's immediate surroundings. AP-relative geometry derived from surveyed AP coordinates, by contrast, encodes the global physical relationship between AGV and AP that the gradient-boosted model can exploit directly. The SHAP attribution analysis (Section~\ref{sec:shap}) supports this reading: AP-relative features dominate the model's predictions across every fold, and the per-feature contribution of LiDAR sectoral features is the smallest of any group.

\subsection{Practical implications}
The 10-feature stack ($(x_m, y_m)$ + 3 telemetry features + 5 AP-relative features) achieves an RMSE of $3.96\dB$ on \texttt{signal\_power} in the within-session LRO evaluation under depth-4 hyperparameters --- approximately 1\,dB below the 0.5-m position-binning noise floor of 4.95\,dB. The model is XGBoost with depth-4, $\eta=0.05$, $\lambda=1.0$, $n_\text{est}=2000$, early-stop $= 100$; anomaly handling is a single threshold $T^{\star} = 35$ on \texttt{nns\_position\_confidence}. LiDAR-derived environment features did not contribute to either cross-session generalization (LiDAR removable on the F1 and F2 folds; F3's nominal positive diagnosed as marginal-distribution-driven) or within-session generalization under proper testing (no fold's $\dlidarw$ both cleared the threshold and survived a deployment-relevant correction). For the studied deployment scenario, this implies that LiDAR sensor-fusion engineering for link quality prediction, sectoral feature pipelines, and in-FOV/out-of-FOV stratified models with LiDAR-derived FOV did not add value over the 10-feature AP-aware baseline.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations bound the scope of this paper's claims.
\textit{Single facility, single AP, three sessions.} The experiments are conducted on one AGV in one industrial facility, across three routes (one in a different sub-area); cross-facility replication is left to future work. 
\textit{Single front LiDAR.} The AGV has a single front-facing 270$^{\circ}$ LiDAR (1{,}350 active beams, 222$^{\circ}$ empirical valid sector), whereas production AGVs typically carry front+rear LiDARs; the in-FOV stratum used throughout is the dual-LiDAR-equivalent lower bound, and the negative result holds in both strata. 
\textit{Single independently-aligned reference frame.} F3's nominal $+1\dB$ $\Delta_\mathrm{LiDAR}$ is diagnosed as marginal-distribution-driven (Section~\ref{sec:f3-diag}); a definitive evaluation requires additional sessions on independently-mapped routes.
\textit{Spatial autocorrelation.} The S1 semivariogram range ($\approx 20$\,m) is comparable to the within-session region size and a 1\,m buffer does not fully decorrelate, so the absolute within-session RMSE values within a fold should be read as fold-conditional rather than as deployment-grade generalization estimates --- though the $\dlidarw$ contrast is not inflated by this alone (Section~V-B). 
\textit{Hyperparameter dependence.} The H0 depth-6 trees overfit on some folds (notably R-1, where the depth-6 model collapses sharply on the held-out region); the lowest-RMSE deployment-relevant numbers come from depth-4. The negative result holds across all tested hyperparameter configurations, but is more decisive at depth-4.
\textit{SHAP is descriptive, not causal.} The SHAP analyses describe the trained models' learned relationships, not underlying physical causes. 
\textit{Single-threshold anomaly handling.} The histogram-saddle threshold $T^{\star} = 35$ is applied uniformly across the three sessions; per-session adaptive thresholds were not evaluated.


\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presents a use-case study that tests whether LiDAR-derived environmental geometry can improve Wi\nobreakdash-Fi signal strength prediction for industrial AGVs over what AP-relative features alone achieve. The study used cross-session and within-session generalization protocols on 681{,}593 matched LiDAR--telemetry pairs from three data-gathering sessions covering three routes. Across the experiments --- spanning cross-session generalization, hyperparameter and framework sensitivity, within-session generalization with spatial and overfitting controls, and LiDAR-feature permutation controls --- ego-frame LiDAR features did not contribute robust value over a 10-feature baseline of position, telemetry, and AP-relative geometry derived from surveyed AP coordinates. The single fold to nominally clear the $+1\dB$ threshold (F3, $+1.20\dB$; trained and tested across independently-aligned coordinate reference frames) was diagnosed by the permutation control as marginal-distribution-driven rather than row-aligned LiDAR signal. The lowest-RMSE within-session LiDAR-free model achieves an RMSE of $3.96\dB$ on \texttt{signal\_power}, approximately 1\,dB below the dataset's 0.5-m position-binning noise floor. The contributions are (i) a controlled empirical study of LiDAR-augmented Wi\nobreakdash-Fi signal strength prediction in this deployment scenario, (ii) a feature group ablation procedure, and (iii) the identification of a minimal 10-feature set that achieves sub-noise-floor within-session prediction in the studied scenario. Future work includes cross-facility replication, dual-LiDAR coverage, additional sessions on independently-aligned reference frames, and pose-uncertainty modeling.
